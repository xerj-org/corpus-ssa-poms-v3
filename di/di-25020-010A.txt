SSA Program Operations Manual System (POMS) - DI 25020.010 - Mental Limitations
Effective Dates: 06/21/2024 - Present | TN 8 (07-24)
Source: https://secure.ssa.gov/apps10/poms.nsf/lnx/0425020010 (fetched 2026-10-04)

A. Policy
1. Nonexertional vs. exertional
Mental limitations are generally nonexertional, but some mental impairments (e.g.,
depression and conversion disorders) may also limit exertion.
2. Medical listing not met or equaled
a.
It cannot be assumed that a failure to meet or equal one of the medical listings for
mental impairments equates with the capacity to do at least unskilled work.
b.
If a medical listing is not met or equaled, the adjudicator consider whether the individual
can meet the mental demands of past relevant work (PRW) and, if not, whether they
have the ability to adjust to other work considering their remaining mental and other
functional capacities and vocational factors.
For information on using the SSA-2506-BK (Psychiatric Review Technique (PRT)) to evaluate steps 2 and 3 of the sequential
evaluation process, see DI 24583.005.
3. Mental demands of unskilled work
a.
The basic mental demands of competitive, remunerative, unskilled
work include the abilities (on a sustained basis) to:
•
Understand, carry out, and remember simple instructions;
•
Make judgments that are commensurate with the functions of unskilled work (i.e., simple
work-related decisions).
•
Respond appropriately to supervision, coworkers, and work situations; and
•
Deal with changes in a routine work setting.
b.
A substantial loss of ability to meet any of the basic mental
demands listed in DI 25020.010A.3.a, in this section.
•
Severely limits the potential occupational base and thus,
•
Would justify a finding of inability to perform other work even for persons with favorable
age, education, and work experience.
c.
A person who can meet all of the mental demands listed in DI 25020.010A.3.a, in this section, and has only a mental limitation(s) will almost always be capable
of adjusting to other work since their potential occupational base would be the unskilled
jobs at all exertional levels.
NOTE: “Substantial loss” cannot be precisely defined. It does not necessarily relate to any particular adjective,
number, or percentage. In practical terms, an individual has a substantial loss of
ability to perform a basic mental activity when they cannot perform the particular
activity in regular, competitive employment but, at best, could do so only in a sheltered
work setting where special considerations and attention are provided. This requires
professional judgment, on the basis of the evidence in the file in each case. The
impairment in a claim of this type may meet or equal the listed medical criteria.
Therefore, before making a determination that includes vocational evaluation, the
adjudicator should discuss the case with the psychological consultant (PC) to learn
whether a significant part of the evidence had previously been overlooked or underrated.
EXCEPTION: In some instances where a person’s vocational profile is extremely adverse (e.g., where the person meets the lifetime commitment medical-vocational
profile because they are closely approaching retirement age, have a limited education
or less, and have essentially a lifetime commitment to a field of unskilled work that is now precluded by a mental impairment), a finding of “disabled” may be appropriate even though the person has only a mental limitation(s) and can
meet all of the demands listed in DI 25020.010A.3.a, in this section. For more information on when the lifetime commitment medical-vocational
profile applies, see DI 25010.001B.3.
