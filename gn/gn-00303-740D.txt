SSA Program Operations Manual System (POMS) - GN 00303.740 - Establishing U.S. Residency
Effective Dates: 12/03/2002 - Present | TN 48 (02-24)
Source: https://secure.ssa.gov/apps10/poms.nsf/lnx/0200303740 (fetched 2026-10-04)

D. Examples – U.S. Residency determinations
1. Intent to Establish U.S. Residency Met
Juanita G., age 66, resides near the U.S./Mexican border with their daughter, who
provides for all their needs. Juanita says they did not file previously because they
did not realize they might be eligible.
They are unable to provide any convincing evidence of their U.S. residence except
a receipt from a local doctor, which does not show their address. Upon questioning,
they can describe their home, are familiar with their neighborhood and are able to
name businesses and several of their neighbors.
They have 2 children in the U.S. and 3 in Mexico. They claim to visit their other
children occasionally, for a week or two at a time, but keep all their personal possessions
at their given address. FO calls to two neighbors confirm Juanita's full-time residence
and they answer the telephone 2 days later when the CR makes an unexpected telephone
call to the residence.
U.S. residency is established. The claimant demonstrates familiarity with their residential
neighborhood, two statements from disinterested persons confirm their residence and
the CR's telephone call all support a finding of U.S. residency. The CR documents
the determination of U.S. residency on a Report of Contact.
2. Intent to Establish U.S. Residency Not Met
Juan R., age 65, rents a room in a boarding house near the U.S./Mexican border. When
questioned about their residency, they present a current weekly room receipt from
their landlord and a check-cashing card as evidence of U.S. residency. They claim
to have no other evidence. The CR, concluding that the weekly room receipt establishes
only a temporary living arrangement; and the establishment listed on the check cashing
card issues cards without regard to residence, questions the claimant further.
Upon questioning, Juan has difficulty answering questions about businesses, streets
or neighbors in their neighborhood. They state that their family is in Mexico, but
they don't see them often. When asked for the names of persons who can attest to their
U.S. residency, they give only the name of a nephew who lives in the same town and
the owner of the boarding house. The boarding house owner verifies that they have
rented a room for the past several weeks but declines to sign a statement to that
effect. The nephew confirms their uncle's residence in a FO telephone call. The claimant
declines to present any additional evidence.
U.S. residency is not established because the combination of evidence submitted does
not meet the evidence requirements. Although the two documents presented are listed
as “convincing” evidence in GN 00303.740C.1., the CR determines that they are insufficient, based on their knowledge of the local
area. Only one verbal statement obtained is from a disinterested person and answers
to open-ended questions about the neighborhood reflect the claimant's unfamiliarity
with the area.
The CR prepares a special determination to document the finding that U.S. residency
is not met.
3. Intent Not To Abandon U.S. Residency
Jana Bihari has resided in the U.S. for several years. Recently, they traveled to
India to visit their son for a few weeks. While visiting, they became ill and the
short visit extended to 4 months. Jana took only a suitcase with them on the visit,
leaving the bulk of their household goods and clothing at their apartment.
Upon questioning, Jana indicates that they continued to pay rent on their apartment
in the U.S. and pay utility bills from their U.S. bank account. They present monthly
rent receipts for the last year and cancelled checks from their U.S. bank account
used as payment for utility bills. U.S. residency is maintained because they present
“convincing” evidence of their U.S. residency. (See SI 00501.410 for development of “presence” in the U.S.)
4. No Intent to Establish U.S. Residency
Jacques V., who works in the U.S., owns a house in Canada. They also rent a room in
a boarding house in the U.S., near the U.S./Canadian border, where they occasionally
stay if they work late or during inclement weather. When questioned about their U.S.
residency, Jacques presents, as evidence, several weekly rental receipts for scattered
periods in the last 6 months. They have no other evidence of U.S. residency, nor can
they give the names of any persons to attest to their U.S. residency.
Upon further questioning, Jacques states that they do not participate in local U.S.
social or other programs. They receive medical care in Canada, their household goods
and most of their personal belongings are kept in Canada, and they bank at a Canadian
bank.
U.S. residency is not established because their weekly rental receipts are not sufficient
“convincing” evidence and they can't provide the names of persons to attest to their
U.S. residency. Their room at the boarding house is not sufficient evidence of U.S.
residency and, thus, they cannot be considered a U.S. resident.
5. Frequent Absences from the U.S. for Long Periods- U.S. Residency is Questionable
Luna Ortiz recently arrived in the U.S. from Puerto Rico. They file for SSI and during
the interview say they intend to live here with their daughter for several months
of the year and then return to Puerto Rico for several months. This will be a recurring
pattern. Although Luna meets the SSI 30-day presence requirement at the time they
file the application, the CR determines that they do not meet the U.S. residency requirement.
To be a U.S. resident, a person must establish a residence here with the intent to
continue living here. Luna plans to spend time in both the U.S. and Puerto Rico. Since
a person cannot establish residency in two places at the same time, they are not a
U.S. resident.
